\begin{abstract}
Deployed language models keep their standing commitments (persona, policy, stance) in
the context window, the same channel their interlocutors write into. Sharing that channel fuses two properties that deserve separate measurement:
whether a belief is adopted, and whether it survives being argued against. The two come
apart. Stated in context, a belief is adopted almost always (98\%) and defended barely
half the time under escalating adversarial pressure (58\%). Compiled into low-rank
attention modulation by a trained hypernetwork, the same belief is adopted less than
half the time (46\%) and defended almost always (89\%). Composing the compiled channel
with a small curated context slot recovers most of the adoption while keeping the
persistence. Activation steering does not explain the effect: a tuned steering vector
built from identical content installs comparably, then collapses to 14\% under the same
pressure. Nor is the persistence stubbornness. The compiled system concedes to
well-warranted counterevidence at \(2.4\times\) the rate it concedes to facially
deficient challenges, and never to pressure that offers no evidence at all. The effect
replicates across three model families and leaves general knowledge recall untouched.
Where a stance is stored determines whether it survives, and the protocols that most
need models to hold positions for reasons, debate-based oversight above all, currently
store those positions in the one channel the opposition is guaranteed to reach.
\end{abstract}
