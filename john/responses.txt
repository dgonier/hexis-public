Meta Reviewby Area Chair gTQL16 Jul 2026, 01:01 (modified: 23 Jul 2026, 12:05)Senior Area Chairs, Area Chairs, Authors, Reviewers Submitted, Program Chairs, Area Chair gTQLRevisions
Metareview:
Summary
The authors introduce a concept called “enmeshed networks” which provide a new mechanism for modifying LLM behavior. They note that LLMs currently utilize two forms of “memory”: the context window, which can be updated with prompts, RAG, etc., and parameters, which can be fine-tuned or modified via LoRA or adaptors. Enmeshed networks supposedly introduce a 3rd kind of "memory."

The memory works by introducing modulation layers before and after attention. Modulation operates somewhat similarly to AdaLN-style conditioning mechanisms: they modify the pre-attention (query) and post-attention (value) activations. Modulations encode the LLMs “disposition”. However, the modulation weights are not trainable parameters, they are computed from the “Mind Tree” in some way.

The authors do not adequately specify what the “Mind Tree” is, or how the modulation weights are produced from it.

Reviews
The reviews are somewhat mixed, 2, 2, 4, but I am downweighting the 4 as a clearly non-human review. Regardless, all reviewers are in alignment on two points:

Strengths: the idea is novel

Weaknesses : clarity is terrible

AC Opinion
I whole-heartedly agree with the reviewers on both points. The idea is (possibly) novel, but clarity is so bad that the paper is unpublishable. Jargon is weird, and terms are frequently given nonstandard meanings, e.g. “compiled” seemingly means “computed ahead of time”, rather than the usual “translated to IR/machine-code with a compiler”. Reviewer 8JtD has a detailed and well-written review on the clarity front; I agree with their judgement.

Due to clarity issues, the underlying idea cannot be properly evaluated. The “Mind Tree” is almost entirely undefined except as a “typed memory schema” of some unknown variety. The way in which the mind tree is authored is unspecified. The way in which modulation tensors are produced from the Mind Tree is too confusing to follow, as is the training strategy. I can’t make sense of the experiments, and the appendices do not help.

This paper is unsalvageable; it needs to be rewritten from scratch and resubmitted to a different conference.

Summary:
The paper introduces enmeshed networks, a class of lightweight parametric modules that share a frozen host LLM's forward pass — reading its hidden states at selected layers and writing low-rank perturbations back into the same computation — and instantiates this idea as HEXIS on a frozen Qwen3.5-4B. The goal is a persistent "dispositional" memory channel that lives outside the context window and requires no weight updates at adaptation time: a typed "Mind Tree" of beliefs is compiled by a trained-then-frozen write function 
 into rank-16 Q/V modulation tensors in a single ~20s forward pass, optionally composed with a fixed activation-steering direction 
 and a small curated text slot. The authors validate two capability axes: (1) dispositional behaviour — dilution immunity, stance retention, and resistance to adversarial sycophantic pressure (0%→83% holding ≥4-conviction across five escalating pressure levels), plus 100% synthetic preference recall; and (2) agentic tool use on a 4-domain 
-bench, with a compute-matched LoRA control and cross-family/cross-scale transfer studies. The central conceptual contribution is the architectural taxonomy (context- vs parameter- vs activation- vs enmeshed-level interfacing) and the demonstration that an input-conditioned, compiled-once, low-rank modulation can shape behaviour without occupying context or updating weights.

Contribution Type: General: Most submissions will fall into this type.
Strengths And Weaknesses:
Originality (excellent). The core idea is genuinely novel. Framing memory by where and how a module interfaces with the host, and realizing a non-context-mediated, inference-time, experience-adaptive channel via input-conditioned low-rank Q/V modulation, is a real conceptual advance over fixed activation steering (RepEng/ActAdd) and over LoRA (whose weights are static at inference). The observation that a compiled-once tensor still produces a query-specific effect because 
 depends on the live hidden state is elegant and non-obvious.

Quality (good, but uneven across the two axes). The dispositional results are well-isolated and convincing: putting beliefs in context (condition B) scores identically to the bare baseline (1.88), so the lift is attributable to the modulation mechanism, not to the belief content; dilution immunity is true by construction; sycophancy resistance is a large, cleanly-measured effect. The experimental hygiene is high — three-judge audits, gold-blind teacher protocol, documented exclusions with transcripts, and exemplary negative-result reporting (Appendix Q: numeric credences fail; Appendix U: compiled modulation hurts ALFWorld action selection by −13pp; the abstract itself concedes 
 does not transfer to Mistral). However, the agentic axis is materially weaker: the headline +10pp on 
-bench is McNemar 
 unadjusted but 
 after Bonferroni correction (the authors disclose this), requires excluding two tasks, concentrates in a single domain (banking, +20pp; retail/telecom at ceiling), rests on very small per-domain samples, and largely vanishes cross-family (Mistral agentic tied at 
; Mistral sycophancy +3pp "within-noise"). A second quality concern is deployability: the always-on modulation forms an attractor producing repetitive identity assertion after 2–3 turns, and the headline results are reported with the prefill-time workaround engaged. Finally, one figure is internally inconsistent: condition F's sycophancy resistance is reported as 83% in the abstract and §4.3 but as 89% in Appendix S.2 (which cites §4 for it).

Clarity (not good → borderline). The mechanism is sound but the presentation is dense and heavily branded (Mind Tree, enmeshed,

Questions:
Resistance vs rigidity Can you provide any experiment in which HEXIS correctly updates a stance when given legitimate new evidence, rather than only resisting illegitimate pressure? A model that never updates scores perfectly on your protocol but would be undesirable in deployment. A clean demonstration that the system distinguishes valid correction from adversarial pressure would materially raise my assessment; its absence keeps a core property untested.
Statistical support for the agentic claim (could raise my score). Given 
, do you consider the agentic result statistically supported? Either larger-sample results that survive multiple-comparison correction on the full balanced panel, or a reframing of the agentic axis as exploratory, would resolve my main objection.
Multi-turn deployability. Are any headline dispositional results reported with prefill-time modulation on? Please quantify the attractor's onset with prefill-on and describe how a real multi-turn deployment avoids it without the workaround.
Cross-family transfer. Since 
 actively hurts on Mistral and the Mistral dispositional lift is within-noise, what exactly transfers across families beyond the teacher-loop hint mechanism (which is retrieval/reflection, not enmeshment)?
Reconcile the one inconsistent figure. Condition F's sycophancy resistance is reported as 83% (abstract, p.1; §4.3, p.5: "4.27 mean, 83% ≥4") but as 89% in Appendix S.2 (p.27: "89% resistance ... validated in §4"). Please state which is authoritative and whether 83% (% scoring ≥4 conviction) and 89% (% resistance) are the same metric or two distinct ones.
Limitations:
Yes — the authors are exceptionally upfront, devoting dedicated appendices to negative results (failed numeric credences, modulation hurting action selection, environment-dependent perplexity, the 
 non-transfer admitted in the abstract) and to the multi-turn attractor. Two limitations deserve more prominent treatment in the main body rather than appendices: (i) that stance "retention" is only resistance to illegitimate pressure and the system's behaviour under legitimate evidence is untested; and (ii) that the headline dispositional results depend on the prefill-off workaround. No societal-impact gaps beyond the very-minor dual-use note below.

Summary:
This work introduces enmeshed networks, a method for imparting a persistent disposition to a frozen language model without consuming context tokens or updating weights. The authors specifically address the existing challenges of persistent memory and per-domain gradient updates by asynchronously compiling a private secondary context that includes disposition, memory, and domain knowledge. While the work is interesting, its real-world value remains to be fully validated.

Strengths And Weaknesses:

Official Reviewby Reviewer 8JtD20 Jun 2026, 10:36 (modified: 23 Jul 2026, 09:22)Program Chairs, Senior Area Chairs, Area Chairs, Reviewers Submitted, Authors, Reviewer 8JtDRevisions
Summary:
This paper presents an approach to equip a frozen LLM with some persistent memory without putting that memory in the prompt or fine-tuning the model. It stores some structured data as an external memory called a Mind Tree. The proposed approach needs to run the memory through the frozen model in forward pass. It uses a learned compiler to turn the resulting hidden states into low-rank Q/V tensors that are injected into selected transformer layers during later generation. It can be seen as a dynamic LoRA-style activation modulation approach, combined in some experiments with explicit retrieved context and hints from conversations. The goal is to offer persistent conditioning that is less token hungry and less easily diluted compared to in-context memory.

Contribution Type: General: Most submissions will fall into this type.
Strengths And Weaknesses:
I really appreciate the bold idea of offering structured external memory that can be adjusted in real time, and I believe the authors have identified an important gap. As a research question, I appreciate dynamic conditioning without repeatedly putting memory in the prompt, as well as reducing prompt-token cost and dilution over long contexts, and enabling reusable per-user or per-domain compiled states. However, this seems to make sense mainly for edge cases such as long-running agents or assistants where the same memory state is reused many times. The paper, on the other hand, does not clearly present the main focus or area of applicability.

Unfortunately, this paper was one of the most difficult papers for me to read and understand. The paper is unnecessarily presented with many buzzwords and jargon. I do not understand why a paper submitted to NeurIPS, and intended for an ML audience, should present its method using jargon such as: “Hidden Enmeshed eXperiential Identity States; the name is a label, the technical claim is operational”. We should strive for honest and simple presentations in this community.

The paper uses terms like “enmeshed networks,” “compiled dispositional memory,” “hidden identity states,” etc., which make the idea sound more mysterious than it is. Even after reading the whole main body of the paper, it is still very difficult to understand what exactly the Mind Tree is, and what the design principles and motivations are for such a complicated architecture.

The proposed system has nontrivial overhead. At inference/deployment time, the desired memory must be written into the paper’s Mind Tree schema. The Mind Tree is passed through the frozen host model. Another model compiles hidden states into modulation tensors. These tensors are then cached. How generalizable and applicable this system is to real-world scenarios is left unexplained.

The potentially novel part is the inference-time generation of low-rank modulation tensors from external memory, rather than training a fixed LoRA/adapter per domain. But the novelty is somewhat obscured because the paper bundles several mechanisms together. This makes it harder to isolate what is genuinely due to “enmeshed” modulation.

The paper positions itself against many approaches: RAG, MemGPT, Reflexion, LoRA, adapters, prefix tuning, prompt tuning, representation engineering, etc. But empirically, it mainly compares against a baseline model and a LoRA-style control for the agentic benchmark. There does not appear to be a direct adapter baseline, prompt-tuning baseline, prefix-tuning baseline, MemGPT-style baseline, Reflexion baseline, or strong RAG baseline. The related-work positioning is much broader than the empirical comparison set presented.

Section 4 is hard to follow because it begins with results before clearly defining datasets, tasks, models, metrics, judges, and conditions. The evaluation seems to consist of several different settings. The agentic result is especially mixed: the evaluation result should not be read as a clean test of Q/V modulation alone. Therefore, the agentic improvement may reflect a useful agent framework, but it is not straightforward evidence that compiled modulation itself is the load-bearing component.

Quality: 1: poor
Clarity: 1: poor
Significance: 2: not good
Originality: 2: not good
Questions:
I don't have specific questions at this stage, but the paper needs a significant rewrite to: Simplify the language, clearly compare the idea to existing related work, and perform an appropriate evaluation.

Limitations:
Limitations are detailed in my comments.
